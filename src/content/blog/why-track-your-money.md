Most of us have a rough idea of what we earn. Far fewer know where it goes. Money leaves in small
pieces: a ride here, a food order there, a load top-up, a transfer to family. Each one feels too
small to matter, and by the end of the month the total is a surprise.

Tracking your money closes that gap. It isn't about cutting out everything you enjoy. It's about
seeing your spending clearly enough to decide what you actually want to do with it.

## What you get from tracking

**You find the leaks.** Small, regular payments add up quietly. When you see a whole month in one
place, the categories that grow without you noticing (deliveries, rides, subscriptions you forgot
about) stand out right away.

**Saving stops being a guess.** "I'll save whatever is left" rarely leaves anything. Once you know
what a normal month costs, you can set aside a fixed amount on payday and know it's realistic.

**You worry less.** Not knowing is often more stressful than the numbers themselves. A clear
picture, even an uncomfortable one, gives you something you can act on.

**You catch mistakes.** Reading your transactions every month is how you notice a double charge, a
payment you don't recognise, or a subscription that renewed when you meant to cancel it.

**Your goals get a timeline.** Whether it's an emergency fund, a new phone or school fees, knowing
how much you can put aside each month tells you when you'll get there.

**Records are ready when you need them.** If you freelance or sell online, a monthly record of what
came in makes it much easier to answer questions about your income later, without digging through
months of notifications.

## Why most people give up

Tracking usually fails for one reason: it takes too much effort. Writing down every purchase in a
notebook or an app works for a week, then life gets busy.

The good news is that you don't need to record anything by hand. If you pay with GCash, Maya or a
bank card, your statement already lists every transaction with its date and amount. The work is
already done; you just need it in a form you can sort and add up.

## A simple monthly habit (about 20 minutes)

**1. Get last month's statement.** Download your transaction history or statement as a PDF from
your wallet or bank.

**2. Turn it into a spreadsheet.** Use the free [statement converter](/) to get an Excel or CSV
file. It runs on your own device, so your statement isn't uploaded anywhere. Each transaction is
given a category automatically (Food, Transport, Bills, Shopping and so on), and you can change any
of them before you download.

**3. Look at the totals by category.** The converter shows money in and money out per category,
and the Excel file has the same totals on its Summary sheet. Ask yourself one question: does this
match what I thought I was spending?

**4. Pick one thing to change.** Not ten. Maybe it's fewer delivery orders, or cancelling a
subscription you don't use. One change you keep beats a strict budget you abandon.

**5. Set your savings for next month.** Based on what a normal month really costs, decide how much
to move to savings on payday, before you start spending.

Repeat next month. After two or three months you'll know your own patterns better than any budget
template could tell you.

## Tips that make it stick

- **Keep categories few.** Eight to ten is plenty. More than that and you'll spend your time
  sorting instead of learning anything.
- **Separate transfers from spending.** Money you move between your own accounts, or set aside for
  savings, isn't an expense. Give it its own category so it doesn't inflate your spending.
- **Compare months, not days.** A single expensive day means little. The trend over a few months
  tells you what's really happening.
- **Be kind to yourself.** The point is to see clearly, not to feel guilty. Every month you track
  is better information than the month before.
